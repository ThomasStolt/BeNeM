## The problem, stated precisely

A customer runs their own BeNeM middleware against their own BHNM. They install the BeNeM iOS app
**from the App Store** — Thomas's build, signed by Thomas's team.

**An APNs authentication key is bound to the publishing team, and the notification topic is the
app's bundle id.** A customer's middleware holds neither. It therefore cannot push to a device
running Thomas's build, no matter how correctly it is configured. This is not a gap in BeNeM; it
is how APNs works.

Three ways out exist, and only one survives:

| | what it means | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Customer publishes their own build | their team, their key, their App Store listing | Works, and is a real option for large customers. Requires an Apple Developer account, review, and a separate app per customer. Not a product. |
| Share the `.p8` with customers | they push directly as Thomas's app | **Rejected — see below** |
| A relay Thomas operates | customer middleware → relay → APNs → device | **The design.** Precedent: Nextcloud and Home Assistant both run exactly this. |

### Why sharing the `.p8` is rejected outright

An APNs auth key signs pushes for **every app in the team**. Handing it to a customer hands them
the ability to push arbitrary notifications to every user of every app Thomas publishes, forever,
until the key is revoked — and revoking it breaks every customer at once.

**Topic-specific keys reduce this and do not remove it.** They narrow the blast radius to one
bundle id, which is still every BeNeM user on earth. The failure mode is a customer's compromised
server becoming a push channel into every BeNeM installation, and no amount of contractual
language makes that recoverable.

Not a close call, recorded so it is not revisited.

## Android does not need this, and saying so changes the scope

Web Push has **no vendor binding**. VAPID keys are generated by whoever runs the server, and the
browser accepts them. A self-hosting customer's middleware can push to their own Android users
today, with no relay, no Thomas, and no shared secret.

So the relay is **an iOS-only construct**, and the product asymmetry is:

- **Android self-hosters:** fully independent. Nothing to build.
- **iOS self-hosters:** dependent on a relay Thomas operates, or on publishing their own app.

That asymmetry should be stated plainly in the docs rather than discovered. It also sets the
relay's true cost floor: it exists to serve one platform's constraint.

## The two variants

### (a) Plaintext relay — rejected as the target

The customer's middleware sends the finished notification (title, body, incident id) to the relay,
which forwards it to APNs.

Simple, and it makes Thomas a **processor of every customer's operational data**: hostnames,
service names, incident text, outage timing — a live feed of when each customer's infrastructure
is broken and what it is called. That is a meaningful security and legal position to hold on
behalf of people who chose self-hosting *specifically to avoid* it, and it turns the relay into a
target worth attacking.

### (b) Encrypted payload + Notification Service Extension — the target

The customer's middleware encrypts the notification content to a key **only the device holds**.
The relay forwards an opaque blob. An iOS **Notification Service Extension** decrypts it on the
device before display.

The relay then sees: a device token, a tenant id, a blob, and a size. It cannot read hostnames or
incident text, and cannot manufacture a plausible notification.

```
customer middleware ──encrypt──► relay ──APNs──► device ──NSE decrypts──► notification
   (holds the key)            (blob only)                  (holds the key)
```

**This is the variant to design against.** The plaintext one is a fallback only if the NSE path
proves unworkable on a measurement, and that measurement has not been made.

## What the relay still sees, and why it matters anyway

Encryption is not a full answer and the design must not pretend otherwise:

- **The device token.** Whoever holds it can push *something* to that device. The relay can
  therefore spam or annoy, even if it cannot read. There is no way around this — the relay must
  hold tokens to do its job.
- **Timing and volume.** When a customer's estate is noisy is visible without reading anything.
- **Tenant identity.** By construction.

State these in the customer-facing documentation. A relay described as "we can't see anything" is
a claim that fails on contact with the first person who reads the protocol, and the doctrine about
unverified claims applies to marketing copy as much as to a badge.

## Key distribution, which is the real design question

The encrypted variant lives or dies on getting a key to both the customer's middleware and the
device without the relay ever holding it.

**BeNeM already has the mechanism.** Onboarding is a QR carrying an AES-GCM encrypted blob
(`benem://configure?p=…`), generated by the customer's own admin portal and scanned by the device.
That is exactly a channel from the customer's server to the device that bypasses the relay.

Sketch, not a specification:

1. The customer's admin portal generates a per-device (or per-server) symmetric key.
2. The key rides in the existing QR payload, alongside the other connection material.
3. The device stores it in the Keychain and shares it with the NSE via an **app group**.
4. The middleware encrypts each notification body with it; the relay forwards the ciphertext.

**Open questions that must be settled before any build:**

- **Per-device or per-server key?** Per-device is stronger and makes rotation granular; per-server
  is far simpler and matches how the QR works today. This is a real decision, not a detail.
- **Rotation.** The S1 work has just spent a week establishing that a credential without a
  rotation story is a liability. Do not repeat it here: the design needs an overlap window from
  the start, not as a follow-on.
- **NSE payload limits.** APNs payloads are capped (4 KB, 5 KB for some types). Incident text plus
  ciphertext overhead must fit, and BHNM `{OUTPUT}` can be long. **Unmeasured.**
- **What the NSE shows when decryption fails.** It must not show the raw blob, and it must not
  show nothing. A notification saying "BeNeM: encrypted alert could not be read" is bad; silence
  is worse, because silence is what a healthy night looks like. This is the coverage finding
  (item 11) in another costume, and it needs the same care.

## Operating it, which costs more than building it

The code is the small part. What an operated relay actually requires:

- **Per-tenant quotas and rate limits.** Without them one customer's misconfigured BHNM — the
  alarm storm the delivery-queue work already anticipates — degrades delivery for everyone else.
  The bound belongs at the tenant, not globally.
- **The customer's middleware must queue and retry.** The relay will be unavailable sometimes.
  A page dropped because the relay was restarting is a page nobody received, and the customer's
  side is the only place that knows the alert still matters. This is a **requirement on the
  self-hosted middleware**, not on the relay, and it should be written into the self-hosting
  documentation as such.
- **Three-hop diagnostics.** Today's chain is BHNM → middleware → APNs. With a relay it is BHNM →
  customer middleware → relay → APNs → device. When a page does not arrive, the customer needs to
  know *which hop*, and neither Thomas nor the customer can see the whole chain. The existing
  `/api/v1/diagnostics` two-hop signal is the model; this needs its relay-side equivalent and a
  way for the customer to see it.
- **Abuse and takedown.** A tenant token that leaks becomes someone else's push channel. There
  must be a way to disable one tenant without touching the others.
- **It is not free, and that is normal.** Nabu Casa charges a monthly subscription for exactly
  this service for Home Assistant. A relay run at no charge, funded by goodwill, becomes an
  operational obligation with no budget the first time it pages somebody at 3am by failing.
  **Whether this is a paid service is a product decision that should be made before it is built**,
  not after people depend on it.

## What this does NOT change

- **Critical Alerts and Time Sensitive remain out of scope.** `shared/DECISION.md` rules them out
  for BeNeM, and a relay does not revisit that.
- **Android self-hosters are unaffected.** See above.
- **The existing single-tenant deployment is unaffected.** Thomas's own middleware pushes directly
  with his own key and should continue to; the relay is for *other people's* middleware.

## Sequencing, if it is ever approved

1. **Measure the NSE path** on a real device: payload size budget, decryption, app-group key
   sharing, and what the extension does on failure. Nothing else is worth designing until this is
   known to work.
2. Settle the product decision: paid or not, and who is eligible.
3. Settle per-device vs per-server keying **and its rotation**, together.
4. Only then, protocol and build.
